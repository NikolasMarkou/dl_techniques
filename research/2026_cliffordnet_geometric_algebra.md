# CliffordNet: All You Need is Geometric Algebra

Oct 19, 2026 · @Nikolas Markou

### A Technical Guide and Critical Reading of arXiv:2601.06793v2 (Zhongping Ji, 2026)

This guide explains the Clifford Algebra Network (CAN), also called CliffordNet, and checks it against both the paper and the official PyTorch code. Each section states the method, then where the paper's claims hold, where they need qualification, and what the code actually computes. Parameter counts quoted as "computed" were reproduced from the official code and match the paper's figures.

**What the paper proposes.** An isotropic vision backbone whose block replaces the attention-plus-FFN pair with a single "Clifford interaction". A detail stream and a depthwise-convolution context stream are multiplied channel-wise at a few cyclic channel offsets; the products yield sampled bivector (wedge) coefficients and gated product ("dot") terms, which a linear projection fuses, and a gated residual adds the result to the stream. Trained from scratch on CIFAR-100, a 1.4M-parameter model reaches 77.82% and a 2.6M-parameter model 79.05%.

## 1. Core Motivation

### 1.1 The MetaFormer pattern

Most modern vision backbones follow the MetaFormer pattern (Yu et al. 2022): each block stacks a **spatial mixer** that decides where information comes from on top of a **channel mixer** that decides what to compute.

```
Input
  └─> Norm ─> Spatial mixer (attention or convolution) ─> residual
  └─> Norm ─> Channel mixer (FFN / MLP)                ─> residual
Output
```

The paper's starting point is sound: this two-module split is a design convention, not a mathematical necessity. It is also not a new observation. gMLP folds a spatial gating unit into the FFN (Liu et al. 2021), and the Mamba block merges the sequence mixer and a gated MLP into one homogeneous block (Gu and Dao 2023). FFNs also do more than compensate for attention's linearity; in language models they hold much of the learned factual association (Geva et al. 2021).

**CliffordNet's central claim:** if the interaction operator is algebraically complete, a separate FFN becomes unnecessary.

### 1.2 Does attention discard the antisymmetric component?

The paper argues that scaled dot-product attention compresses the relation between two tokens into a scalar that keeps only the symmetric, inner-product part and discards the bivector. That holds only if queries and keys share a projection. With learned projections q = W\_q xᵢ and k = W\_k xⱼ, the score is a general bilinear form:

```latex
q^{\top}k = x_i^{\top} A\, x_j, \qquad A = W_q^{\top} W_k = A_{\mathrm{sym}} + A_{\mathrm{anti}}
```

and its antisymmetric part is exactly a weighted sum of bivector coefficients:

```latex
x_i^{\top} A_{\mathrm{anti}}\, x_j = \sum_{p<r} (A_{\mathrm{anti}})_{pr}\,\big(x_{i,p}\,x_{j,r} - x_{i,r}\,x_{j,p}\big)
```

Each attention head therefore already reads a learned linear functional of both xᵢ · xⱼ and xᵢ ∧ xⱼ. The accurate statement of the bottleneck is **compression, not symmetry**: attention reduces each token pair to one scalar per head, while the value vectors carry the content forward. CliffordNet's alternative keeps many bilinear coefficients per token and lets a learned projection combine them, at the price of restricting interaction to a local neighbourhood.

## 2. Geometric Algebra Foundations

### 2.1 The Clifford algebra Cl(p, q)

For a vector space with basis e₁, …, eₙ, the Clifford algebra is generated by the relation

```latex
e_i e_j + e_j e_i = 2\,\eta_{ij}
```

where η is a diagonal metric with p entries equal to +1 and q equal to −1. CliffordNet uses the Euclidean algebra Cl(D, 0) over the **channel space**: each of the D feature channels is treated as a basis vector, so a token's feature vector is a grade-1 element of an algebra whose dimension grows with network width.

That choice is unusual. Earlier geometric-algebra networks use small, fixed algebras such as Cl(3, 0) or the projective algebra of 3D space, and give each channel a multivector value (Brandstetter et al. 2023; Ruhe et al. 2023; Brehmer et al. 2023). CliffordNet instead identifies the algebra with the feature space itself.

### 2.2 Multivectors and grades

| Grade | Name | Dimension | Geometric meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Scalar | 1 | Magnitude |
| 1 | Vector | D | Directed line segment |
| 2 | Bivector | C(D, 2) | Oriented plane element |
| 3 | Trivector | C(D, 3) | Oriented volume element |
| k | k-vector | C(D, k) | Oriented k-dimensional element |

A general multivector has 2ᴰ components. A **blade** is a k-vector that factors as a wedge of k vectors; for 2 ≤ k ≤ D − 2, most k-vectors are sums of blades rather than blades.

At a typical width the full objects are far out of reach. With D = 128, the algebra has 2¹²⁸ components and the bivector space alone has 8,128. No layer can represent multivectors over the channel space explicitly; CliffordNet samples a small number of bivector coefficients (Section 6).

The product of two vectors contains only grades 0 and 2:

```latex
uv = \underbrace{u \cdot v}_{\text{grade 0}} + \underbrace{u \wedge v}_{\text{grade 2}}
```

### 2.3 Algebraic completeness

> **Definition (from the paper).** An architecture is *algebraically complete under the geometric product* if it explicitly models both the coherence term (scalar, symmetric) and the structural term (bivector, antisymmetric) of the interaction between feature vectors.

The term is the paper's own, not a standard notion in geometric algebra. Two clarifications apply throughout this guide. First, a learned bilinear interaction is not limited to the symmetric part: attention between separately projected queries and keys already contains an antisymmetric component (Section 1.2). Second, Hadamard gating u ⊙ v is symmetric in its arguments but is not the inner product either; it is the diagonal of the outer product u vᵀ, before summation.

## 3. The Three Core Products

### 3.1 Inner product u · v

```latex
u \cdot v = \tfrac{1}{2}(uv + vu) = \sum_{i=1}^{D} u_i v_i
```

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Result | Grade 0 (scalar) |
| Symmetry | Symmetric: u · v = v · u |
| Zero when | u ⊥ v |
| Largest magnitude (fixed norms) | u ∥ v |
| Neural analogues | Cosine similarity; attention scores when query and key projections coincide |

### 3.2 Exterior (wedge) product u ∧ v

```latex
u \wedge v = \tfrac{1}{2}(uv - vu) = \sum_{1 \le i < j \le D} (u_i v_j - u_j v_i)\, e_i \wedge e_j
```

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Result | Grade 2 (bivector) |
| Symmetry | Antisymmetric: u ∧ v = −v ∧ u, so u ∧ u = 0 |
| Zero when | u ∥ v |
| Magnitude | \|u\| \|v\| sin θ, largest when u ⊥ v |
| Neural analogues | Rare as an explicit operation; present implicitly in any learned bilinear form xᵀAy with A ≠ Aᵀ |

In three dimensions the cross product is the dual of the wedge, u × v = −I(u ∧ v) with I the unit pseudoscalar; the wedge generalizes it to any dimension.

### 3.3 Geometric product uv

```latex
uv = u \cdot v + u \wedge v
```

| Property | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Result | Multivector: scalar plus bivector |
| Associative, distributive | Yes |
| Division | A non-null vector has an inverse, v⁻¹ = v / \|v\|², so given uv and a nonzero u, v = u⁻¹(uv). In Euclidean signature every nonzero vector qualifies. |
| Zero divisors | Present among general multivectors: (1 + e₁)(1 − e₁) = 0 |
| What it retains | Both u · v and u ∧ v, hence the norms' product, the angle and the plane |
| What it loses | The individual factors: (λu)(v/λ) = uv, and rotating both vectors together within their plane leaves uv unchanged |

The geometric product is the only one of the three that keeps both the symmetric and antisymmetric parts, and the only one from which a known nonzero factor can be divided out. It is not information-preserving with respect to the pair (u, v): many pairs share the same product.

## 4. The Clifford Interaction Ansatz

### 4.1 Depth as time

The paper models the layer-wise update of the feature field H ∈ ℝ^{h×w×D} as a dynamical system driven by H and a context field C(H) aggregated from it:

```latex
\frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \mathcal{F}\big(H,\ \mathcal{C}(H)\big)
```

Reading residual networks as Euler discretizations of such a system is established (E 2017; Haber and Ruthotto 2017; Lu et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2018). With separate weights per layer, the system is non-autonomous: each step uses a different field. The analogy is to discretization, not to one learned vector field.

### 4.2 The ansatz

```latex
\mathcal{F}(H, \mathcal{C}) := \mathcal{P}\Big(\underbrace{H \cdot \mathcal{C}}_{\text{coherence}} \oplus \underbrace{H \wedge \mathcal{C}}_{\text{structure}}\Big)
```

Here ⊕ is channel-wise concatenation and 𝒫 is a learned linear projection back to ℝᴰ. In the implementation, 𝒫 maps 2|S|·D concatenated features to D (Section 6), so its size grows with the number of shifts.

### 4.3 Why it is claimed to replace the FFN

The paper gives three reasons; each is assessed.

1. **Internalized nonlinearity.** The products are second order, and SiLU gating follows. This holds: multiplicative interactions are expressive and well studied (Jayakumar et al. 2020), and gated FFNs such as SwiGLU rely on the same mechanism (Shazeer 2020).
2. **Structured channel mixing along a ring, with logarithmic path length.** This argument does not bear weight. The detail projection and 𝒫 are dense, so every output channel depends on every input channel within a single block; the shift set decides which bilinear pairs exist, not how far information travels.
3. **Dual information: energy and structural contrast.** An interpretation; Section 6 shows what the two branches compute.

## 5. Context Instantiation

### 5.1 Local context

The paper defines the local context as two stacked 3×3 depthwise convolutions "separated by non-linear activation" and credits them with a 7×7 receptive field. Neither detail matches the code or the arithmetic.

- **Receptive field.** Stacking k convolutions of size 3 gives a receptive field of 2k + 1. Two give **5×5**; three would give 7×7.
- **No nonlinearity in between.** The official block applies the two depthwise convolutions back to back, then BatchNorm and SiLU. Two linear depthwise 3×3 filters compose into one 5×5 depthwise filter whose kernel is the 2D convolution of the two. The factorization saves parameters (18 against 25 per channel); it does not enlarge the field.
- **Not a Laplacian.** The filters are learned and unconstrained. Nothing makes them approximate the discrete Laplacian, so "C ≈ ΔH" is a motivation, not a property.

### 5.2 The self-energy switch λ

```latex
\mathcal{C} = \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{loc}}(H) - \lambda H, \qquad \lambda \in \{0, 1\}
```

| Mode | λ | Context | Paper's interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute | 0 | C\_loc(H) | Energy-preserving flow |
| Differential | 1 | C\_loc(H) − H | High-pass filter that suppresses static self-energy |

In the code, what is subtracted is the detail stream Z\_det, a 1×1 projection of the normalized input, not H itself.

**Differential mode cannot affect the wedge branch.** The wedge is bilinear and antisymmetric, so subtracting the detail stream from the context changes nothing:

```latex
\mathcal{W}_s(H,\ \mathcal{C} - H) = \mathcal{W}_s(H, \mathcal{C}) - \mathcal{W}_s(H, H) = \mathcal{W}_s(H, \mathcal{C})
```

A numerical check over every Lite shift confirms it to machine precision (largest difference 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ in float64). Three consequences follow:

- The roughly 1.4-point gain of differential over absolute mode (Nano 76.41% to 77.82%; Lite 77.63% to 79.05%) must arise through the dot branch, and through the gate that reads its output.
- In a wedge-only model the switch has no effect at all, which is a cheap test of the reported ablations.
- The explanation that differential mode "maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio of structural variation" cannot apply to the structural, wedge branch.

### 5.3 Global context

```latex
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{glo}} = \mathrm{GlobalAvgPool}(H) \in \mathbb{R}^{D}
```

In the code, the global context is the spatial mean of the normalized input, broadcast to every position, and the global interaction runs in differential mode with shifts {1, 2} in full mode. It adds 4D² + D parameters per block, about 0.79M over 12 blocks, and gains roughly 0.4 to 0.5 points. The readings of its wedge term as saliency and its inner term as semantic coherence are hypotheses the paper does not test.

### 5.4 Dual-scale superposition

The hybrid model sums two separate interactions, each with its own projection:

```latex
\frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{loc}}\big(H\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{loc}}\big) + \beta\, \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{glo}}\big(H\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{glo}}\big), \qquad \beta \in \{0, 1\}
```

The products are full geometric products, not inner products alone, and in the code the two outputs are added before the gate. The paper's shorthand "C = ΔH + β · GlobalAvg(H)" is not what is computed: with separate projections and a SiLU inside the dot branch, the sum of two interactions differs from one interaction with summed context.

## 6. Sparse Rolling: What the Layer Actually Computes

### 6.1 The quadratic problem

All pairwise channel interactions between a token's state h and context c form the outer product M = h cᵀ ∈ ℝ^{D×D}, which costs O(D²) per token. The paper avoids it by sampling diagonals of M with cyclic channel shifts.

### 6.2 The shift operator and the two branches

```latex
[T_s(X)]_{i,c} = X_{i,\,(c+s) \bmod D}
```

For each offset s in a shift set S, the block computes, per position i and channel c:

```latex
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Dot}^{(s)}_{i,c} &= \mathrm{SiLU}\big(H_{i,c}\, C_{i,(c+s) \bmod D}\big) \\
\mathrm{Wedge}^{(s)}_{i,c} &= H_{i,c}\, C_{i,(c+s) \bmod D} - C_{i,c}\, H_{i,(c+s) \bmod D}
\end{aligned}
```

In terms of the outer product, Dot reads the raw entry M\[c, c+s\] through a SiLU, and Wedge reads the antisymmetric difference M\[c, c+s\] − M\[c+s, c\].

### 6.3 The wedge branch is genuine geometric algebra

The wedge term at shift s is exactly the coefficient of the basis bivector `e_c ∧ e_(c+s)` in h ∧ c. It is antisymmetric, vanishes when the two streams coincide, and is computed without approximation. Per token the Lite model samples |S|·D = 640 of the C(128, 2) = 8,128 bivector coefficients, about 8%.

Wrapped indices flip orientation (e\_c ∧ e\_{c+s} with c + s ≥ D is a negatively oriented canonical plane); the paper notes that the projection absorbs the sign, which is correct. Shifts s and D − s index the same planes with opposite sign, so a shift set should not contain both.

### 6.4 The dot branch is not the inner product

The paper calls Dot a "generalized inner product uᵀP\_s v", with P\_s the cyclic permutation matrix. Three properties separate it from the grade-0 term of the geometric product:

| Property | Grade-0 inner product h · c | Dot branch at shift s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output | One scalar per token | D values per token per shift |
| Channels paired | Each channel with itself (shift 0) | Channel c with channel c + s, for s ≥ 1 |
| Summed over channels | Yes | No |
| Symmetric under h ↔ c | Yes | No: `H_c · C_(c+s) ≠ C_c · H_(c+s)` in general |

The bilinear form uᵀPₛv would be the sum of the band; the layer keeps the band's unsummed entries. And because shift sets start at 1, the true scalar term `Σ_c h_c · c_c` never appears. Off-diagonal symmetric combinations do not occur in the geometric product of two vectors at all, since eᵢeⱼ + eⱼeᵢ = 0 for i ≠ j.

What the dot branch does compute is a **sparse, gated sample of the outer product**, the cross-channel generalization of the elementwise gating used in GLU-style layers and convolutional modulation (Section 15). It is useful; it is not geometric algebra. In full mode the layer therefore sees both M\[c, c+s\] and, through the wedge, M\[c+s, c\]: the two diagonals at ±s of the outer product, one of them passed through a SiLU.

### 6.5 Interaction modes

| Mode | Concatenated branches | Width before 𝒫 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| inner | Dot only | \|S\|·D |
| wedge | Wedge only | \|S\|·D |
| full | Wedge and Dot | 2\|S\|·D |

### 6.6 Shift sets

| Shift set | Variant | Parameters | CIFAR-100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {1, 2} | Nano | 1.43M | 77.82% |
| {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} | Lite | 2.61M | 79.05% |

The paper motivates exponential shifts by logarithmic path length across channels. As Section 4.3 notes, dense projections already connect every channel to every other within one block, so the case for a particular shift set is empirical. The parameter difference between Nano and Lite comes entirely from the wider projection, not from the shifts themselves, which have no parameters.

### 6.7 The circulant interpretation, corrected

The paper states that using all shifts would equal multiplication by a circulant matrix, computable in O(D log D) by FFT. Two different objects are being conflated:

- **All shifts, entries kept** (what the layer would compute): every entry of the outer product, D² values per token. No FFT shortcut applies.
- **All shifts, each band summed:** the circular cross-correlation `Σ_c h_c · c_(c+s)`, D values per token, computable in O(D log D) by FFT. That is the generalized inner product uᵀPₛv, but it is not what the layer computes.

"Structured circulant sparsity" is best read as "a fixed sparse set of outer-product diagonals". Section 14 gives the correct cost.

## 7. Gated Geometric Residual (GGR)

A first-order Euler step gives the residual form, with the step size realized as LayerScale γ:

```latex
H_{l} = H_{l-1} + \Delta t \cdot \mathcal{F}(H_{l-1}, \mathcal{C}_{l-1})
```

The refined update, as implemented, with Ĥ = LayerNorm(Hₗ₋₁) and G the projected interaction:

```latex
H_{l} = H_{l-1} + \mathrm{DropPath}\Big(\gamma \odot \big(\mathrm{SiLU}(\hat{H}) + \alpha \odot G\big)\Big), \qquad \alpha = \sigma\big(W_{\mathrm{gate}}\,[\hat{H};\, G]\big)
```

The paper writes SiLU(Hₗ₋₁); the code applies SiLU to the normalized input Ĥ.

| Component | Role |
| --- | --- |
| SiLU(Ĥ) | A pointwise nonlinear copy of the normalized input, added to every update. SiLU attenuates negative values (its minimum is about −0.28) rather than removing them, so "background-noise suppression" is an interpretation. |
| α | Sigmoid gate per position and channel, computed from \[Ĥ; G\]; 2D² + D parameters |
| γ | Per-channel LayerScale (Touvron et al. 2021), initialized to 10⁻⁵ |
| DropPath | Stochastic depth, rising linearly to 0.3 across depth in the Nano and Lite configurations |

The SiLU(Ĥ) path means each block applies a pointwise nonlinearity to its input even when the gate closes the geometric branch. The paper does not ablate this term, so how much of the "No-FFN" performance it carries is unknown.

## 8. The gFFN Family

| Variant | Context, as computed | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| gFFN-L (local) | Local depthwise context minus the detail stream | Nano and Lite (β = 0) |
| gFFN-G (global) | Spatial mean of the normalized input minus the detail stream; shifts {1, 2}, full mode; 4D² + D extra parameters per block | The "+gFFN-G" variants (β = 1) |
| gFFN-H (hybrid) | The sum of the local and global interactions, each with its own projection | Hybrid models |

**What "No-FFN" means.** The models have no separate MLP block, and the paper reports an MLP ratio of 0. They do have FFN-scale channel mixing inside the interaction. Per block in the Lite model:

| Dense map | Shape | Parameters | Share of block |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Detail projection | D → D | D² = 16,384 | 7.6% |
| Interaction projection 𝒫 | 10D → D | 10D² = 163,840 | 76.1% |
| Gate | 2D → D | 2D² = 32,768 | 15.2% |
| Depthwise convolutions | Two 3×3 | 18D = 2,304 | 1.1% |

The three dense maps total 13D² against 8D² for a standard ratio-4 MLP. Structurally, the interaction is an FFN whose hidden layer is 10D wide and whose hidden activations are bilinear products of two streams, one of them spatially mixed, rather than an elementwise nonlinearity of a single linear map. That places it in the gated-FFN family (Dauphin et al. 2017; Shazeer 2020), close to convolutional modulation (Section 15). The accurate claim is that **the FFN is merged into the interaction**, not that it is redundant.

## 9. Full Block Algorithm, as Implemented

```
Input  X: (B, H, W, D)
1  Xn = LayerNorm(X)                                   # over channels
2  Zd = Linear_state(Xn)                               # 1x1, D -> D
3  Zc = SiLU(BN(DWConv3x3(DWConv3x3(Xn))))             # one 5x5 depthwise filter in effect
4  if ctx_mode == "diff": Zc = Zc - Zd                 # leaves every wedge term unchanged
5  feats = []
   for s in S:
       Zd_s, Zc_s = roll(Zd, s), roll(Zc, s)           # along channels
       if mode in ("wedge", "full"): feats += [Zd * Zc_s - Zc * Zd_s]
       if mode in ("inner", "full"): feats += [SiLU(Zd * Zc_s)]
6  G = Linear_proj(concat(feats))                      # |S|*D or 2|S|*D -> D
7  if beta == 1:
       G = G + GlobalInteraction(Zd, spatial_mean(Xn)) # gFFN-G: shifts {1, 2}, full, diff
8  a = sigmoid(Linear_gate(concat(Xn, G)))
9  return X + DropPath(gamma * (SiLU(Xn) + a * G))
```

The paper's pseudo-code appends two tensors in one call for full mode; that is not valid list syntax and should extend the list, as above. Positive shifts in torch.roll and keras.ops.roll move channel c to position c + s, so the code pairs channel c with c − s rather than c + s. The two conventions sample the same planes with opposite orientation, which the projection absorbs.

## 10. Architecture, Variants and Parameter Accounting

### 10.1 Isotropic layout

Unlike hierarchical backbones, CliffordNet keeps one feature-map size through every block. The paper ties this to its view of learning as a flow in a fixed phase space.

```
Image (32x32x3)
   │
Stem: 3x3 conv, stride 2, + BatchNorm      (patch size 2)
   │
Feature map 16x16xD                        fixed through all blocks
   │
CliffordNet block × L
   │
Global average pool → LayerNorm → linear classifier
```

The official repository has since added a hierarchical, pyramid variant evaluated on medical-image datasets; the CIFAR-100 results all use the isotropic layout.

### 10.2 Model variants

Depth, width and mode come from the official configurations where stated. Where marked \*, the value is inferred: a width of 128 reproduces every reported parameter count, and the count does not depend on which shifts are chosen, only on how many.

| Model | Depth L | Width D | Shifts | Mode | Global | Params (paper) | Params (computed) | CIFAR-100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nano | 12 | 128 | {1, 2} | full | No | 1.43M | 1.433M | 77.82% |
| Lite | 12 | 128 | {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} | full | No | 2.61M | 2.613M | 79.05% |
| Nano + gFFN-G | 12 | 128 | {1, 2} | full | Yes | 2.22M | 2.221M | 78.22% |
| Lite + gFFN-G | 12 | 128 | {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} | full | Yes | 3.40M | 3.401M | 79.57% |
| CliffordNet-32 | 32 | 128\* | 3 shifts | full | No | 4.8M | 4.842M | 81.42% |
| CliffordNet-64 | 64 | 128\* | 5 shifts | inner | No | 8.6M | 8.619M | 82.46% |
| CAN-96 (repository only) | 96 | 128\* | 5 shifts\* | inner\* | No | 12.8M | 12.92M | 83.47% |

The suffixes 32 and 64 are **depths**, not channel widths: the paper describes these models as scaling depth, and only a width of 128 reproduces their parameter counts. Note also that the best reported model, CliffordNet-64, uses the dot branch alone, without any wedge term.

### 10.3 Parameter formula

Per block, with m = 2 in full mode and m = 1 in inner or wedge mode:

```latex
P_{\mathrm{block}} = \underbrace{D^2 + D}_{\text{state}} + \underbrace{18D}_{\text{depthwise}} + \underbrace{4D}_{\text{norms}} + \underbrace{m\,|S|\,D^2 + D}_{\text{projection}} + \underbrace{2D^2 + D}_{\text{gate}} + \underbrace{D}_{\gamma} \;\big[+\, 4D^2 + D \text{ with gFFN-G}\big]
```

The stem adds 30D and the head 102D + 100 for 100 classes. The projection term dominates: in the Lite model it is 76% of every block.

### 10.4 Compute

The paper reports parameters and training time but not FLOPs. With patch size 2, every block runs on 256 tokens at full width, and the dense maps give roughly **0.36 GMACs per image for Nano and 0.67 GMACs for Lite** (an estimate from the formula above). That is the same order as the commonly cited figure of about 0.56 GMACs for the CIFAR variant of ResNet-18. Lite has about 4 times fewer parameters than ResNet-18 but similar compute, and in the paper's own timing it trained in 125 minutes against 64.5 for MobileNetV2, before custom kernels.

### 11.3 Reading the comparisons

1. **Check the arithmetic.** Nano beats ShuffleNetV2 1.0× by 3.22 points; the paper's "4.3%" is the relative gain, quoted alongside absolute gaps elsewhere (Lite beats ResNet-18 by 2.30 points). Nano beats ResNet-18 by 1.07 points with one-eighth of the parameters.
2. **Parameter-matched, not compute-matched.** Lite's estimated compute is similar to a CIFAR ResNet-18 (Section 10.4), and it trained about twice as long as MobileNetV2.
3. **Unequal regularization.** Only CliffordNet uses DropPath, at a rate of 0.3.
4. **Baseline construction is unstated.** The paper does not say whether the CNN baselines were adapted to 32×32 inputs (stem stride, pooling), which strongly affects their CIFAR accuracy. Compact transformers built for small datasets, such as CCT (Hassani et al. 2021), and recent efficient CNNs are absent. "State of the art under 3M parameters" is relative to the baselines chosen.
5. **The ViT-Tiny result does not test geometry.** Vision Transformers trained from scratch on small datasets underperform for well-documented reasons: weak locality bias and data hunger (Dosovitskiy et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2021; Hassani et al. 2021). A ViT with its MLP cut to ratio 1.0 on CIFAR-100 says little about "geometric density". A fair control would replace the Clifford interaction with attention or convolutional modulation inside the same backbone, at matched parameters and compute.
6. **No error bars.** Seeds and variance are not reported, so gaps of a few tenths of a point cannot be told apart from run-to-run noise.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Wedge only | 1.63M | 77.76% |

Three corrections to the paper's reading:

- **The comparison is not parameter-matched.** Full mode doubles the projection's input, so the full model has 60% more parameters than either single-branch model. Part of its 0.9-point lead may be capacity. A matched test would give the single-branch models 10 shifts, or the full model about half as many.
- **Neither branch is symmetric, and neither carries "diagonal energy".** The paper's table labels the inner branch symmetric and credits it with same-channel terms of the form uᵢuᵢ. With shifts starting at 1, the dot branch pairs channel c with channel c ± s only and has no same-channel products; the depthwise context is per-channel, so it does not add any either.
- **The finding that survives:** the antisymmetric cross terms alone support 77.76%, within half a point of the dot branch. That is evidence that bivector coefficients are a useful feature, though the strongest model reported, CliffordNet-64, uses the dot branch alone.

## 13. Theoretical Interpretation

### 13.1 The reaction-diffusion reading

| Term | Paper's role | Paper's analogy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H · C (inner) | Anisotropic diffusion | Smoothing where features agree with their context |
| H ∧ C (wedge) | Reaction, "geometric vorticity" | Sharpening at edges, where features turn away from their context |

This is an analogy, not a derivation. A reaction-diffusion system needs a diffusion operator, and the context filter here is learned rather than a Laplacian (Section 5.1); Turing patterns need specific instability conditions that the paper does not establish. "Vorticity" is also loose: the curl of a vector field is bivector-valued, but h ∧ c at one point is the wedge of two vectors at that point, not a derivative of a field. A testable version of the claim would correlate the wedge branch's response with image-gradient magnitude; the paper does not report it.

### 13.2 A more conservative account of why the block works

The block combines four ingredients familiar from strong efficient architectures:

1. **Dense channel mixing** through three linear maps totalling 13D² parameters in Lite.
2. **Multiplicative interaction** between a projected stream and a spatially mixed stream.
3. **Spatial mixing** through a 5×5 depthwise filter.
4. **Pointwise nonlinearity** through SiLU on the context, the dot branch and the residual path.

The results support the claim that a gated bilinear interaction with a convolutional context can replace attention plus MLP on CIFAR-100 at small scale. They do not isolate geometric algebra as the cause; the controls in Section 17 would.

## 14. Complexity Analysis

Per block, for N tokens of width D, with m = 2 in full mode and 1 otherwise:

|  | ViT block | CliffordNet block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Token mixing | O(N²D) attention | O(N·D·k²) depthwise convolution |
| Dense channel maps | O(N·D²): QKV and output (4D²) plus a ratio-4 MLP (8D²) | O(N·(3 + m\|S\|)·D²): state, projection, gate |
| Shifted products |  | O(N·m\|S\|·D), parameter-free |
| Parameters | About 12D² | (3 + m\|S\|)·D² + O(D) |
| Global context | Every layer | Grows with depth; optional global mean |
| Spatial structure | Flattened sequence plus position encoding | Native 2D grid |

The linear-in-N claim holds. The paper's total of O(N·D·|S|) counts only the shifted products and omits the projection, so the true cost is **O(N·(3 + m|S|)·D²)**: quadratic in width and linear in the number of shifts.

**Receptive field.** Each block adds a 5×5 window, so the theoretical field grows by 4 tokens per block. On CIFAR's 16×16 map, four blocks already span the image. At 224 × 224 input with patch size 2, the 112 × 112 map needs about 28 blocks for theoretical global coverage, and effective receptive fields are typically much smaller than theoretical ones (Luo et al. 2016). That matters for the paper's proposed move to dense prediction at high resolution.

## 15. Relation to Prior Work

The paper's related-work section covers physics-inspired attention, Clifford networks for physics, efficient CNNs and state-space models. The block's closest relatives are elsewhere.

| Line of work | Representative papers | Relation to CliffordNet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Geometric-algebra networks | Clifford neural layers (Brandstetter et al. 2023); Clifford group equivariant networks (Ruhe et al. 2023); Geometric Algebra Transformer (Brehmer et al. 2023) | Small fixed algebras with multivector-valued features, aimed at equivariance in physics and 3D. CliffordNet instead treats the channel space as the vector space and samples bivector coefficients; it has no equivariance property. |
| Multiplicative interactions and gated FFNs | Jayakumar et al. 2020; GLU (Dauphin et al. 2017); GLU variants in transformers (Shazeer 2020) | The dot branch at shift 0 would be GLU-style gating; nonzero shifts extend it across channels. |
| Convolutional modulation | Visual Attention Network (Guo et al. 2023); Focal Modulation Networks (Yang et al. 2022); Conv2Former (Hou et al. 2024); HorNet (Rao et al. 2022) | Multiply a projected stream by a depthwise-convolution context, then project: the skeleton of the Clifford block at shift 0. |
| Merged mixer and MLP | gMLP (Liu et al. 2021); the Mamba block (Gu and Dao 2023) | Remove the separate MLP by folding gating into the mixer. |
| Parameter-free shifts | Channel shuffle (Ma et al. 2018); shift operations (Wu et al. 2018) | Cheap data movement in place of mixing parameters. |
| Bilinear features | Compact bilinear pooling (Gao et al. 2016); low-rank bilinear pooling (Kim et al. 2017) | Outer products of features, compressed; sampled diagonals are another structured compression. |
| Residual networks as dynamical systems | E 2017; Haber and Ruthotto 2017; Lu et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2018 | The depth-as-time framing. |
| Transformers for small datasets | Compact Convolutional Transformers (Hassani et al. 2021); Lee et al. 2021 | Stronger attention-based baselines at CIFAR scale. |

Two statements in the paper need correcting against this background:

- **"Rendering the heavy FFN block redundant, a feat not yet demonstrated in CNN or SSM architectures."** The Mamba block already merges the sequence mixer and the gated MLP into one homogeneous block, and gMLP does the analogous merge for vision.
- **Reference attributions.** The paper credits the Geometric Algebra Transformer to Brandstetter, van den Berg, Welling and Gupta; it is by Brehmer, de Haan, Behrends and Cohen. Those four names belong instead to Clifford Neural Layers for PDE Modeling, which the paper credits to Brandstetter, Welling and Worrall.

## 16. Reference Implementation (Keras 3)

A complete block and model in Keras 3, written to match the official PyTorch code. Tested with Keras 3.15 on the JAX backend:

- Trainable parameter counts match the official code exactly: 1.433M (Nano), 2.613M (Lite), 1.630M (Lite, inner only), 2.221M (Nano with gFFN-G).
- Forward passes run in inference and training mode, including stochastic depth.
- A wedge-only interaction gives the same output in differential and absolute mode with shared weights, to float32 rounding (largest difference 1.7 × 10⁻⁶).
- Saving and reloading the model reproduces its outputs exactly.

Design points: all sublayers are created once in the constructor, so weights are reused across calls; the width is passed explicitly; shifts are validated; and the roll direction is documented, since keras.ops.roll pairs channel c with c − s.

```python
"""CliffordNet block in Keras 3, matching the official PyTorch implementation."""
import keras
from keras import layers, ops


@keras.saving.register_keras_serializable(package="cliffordnet")
class CliffordInteraction(layers.Layer):
    """Sparse rolling interaction between a detail stream and a context stream.

    For each shift s, the wedge branch gives the bivector coefficients
    z_det[c] * ctx[c-s] - ctx[c] * z_det[c-s]; the dot branch gives the gated
    outer-product entries SiLU(z_det[c] * ctx[c-s]). keras.ops.roll moves
    channel c to c+s, so channel c is paired with c-s. A dense projection
    maps the concatenated branches back to `dim` channels.
    """

    def __init__(self, dim, shifts=(1, 2), cli_mode="full", ctx_mode="diff", **kwargs):
        super().__init__(**kwargs)
        if cli_mode not in ("inner", "wedge", "full"):
            raise ValueError(f"cli_mode must be inner, wedge or full, got {cli_mode!r}")
        if ctx_mode not in ("diff", "abs"):
            raise ValueError(f"ctx_mode must be diff or abs, got {ctx_mode!r}")
        valid = []
        for s in shifts:
            s = int(s) % dim
            if s != 0 and s not in valid:
                valid.append(s)
        if not valid:
            raise ValueError("shifts must contain at least one value not divisible by dim")
        self.dim, self.shifts = dim, valid
        self.cli_mode, self.ctx_mode = cli_mode, ctx_mode
        self.proj = layers.Dense(dim, name="proj")

    def call(self, z_det, z_ctx):
        ctx = z_ctx - z_det if self.ctx_mode == "diff" else z_ctx
        feats = []
        for s in self.shifts:
            ctx_s = ops.roll(ctx, shift=s, axis=-1)
            if self.cli_mode in ("wedge", "full"):
                det_s = ops.roll(z_det, shift=s, axis=-1)
                feats.append(z_det * ctx_s - ctx * det_s)      # bivector coefficients
            if self.cli_mode in ("inner", "full"):
                feats.append(ops.silu(z_det * ctx_s))         # gated outer-product band
        return self.proj(ops.concatenate(feats, axis=-1))

    def get_config(self):
        return {**super().get_config(), "dim": self.dim, "shifts": self.shifts,
                "cli_mode": self.cli_mode, "ctx_mode": self.ctx_mode}


@keras.saving.register_keras_serializable(package="cliffordnet")
class CliffordBlock(layers.Layer):
    """LayerNorm -> detail and context streams -> Clifford interaction
    (+ optional global interaction) -> gated geometric residual."""

    def __init__(self, dim, shifts=(1, 2), cli_mode="full", ctx_mode="diff",
                 use_global=False, drop_path=0.0, layer_scale_init=1e-5, **kwargs):
        super().__init__(**kwargs)
        self.dim, self.shifts, self.cli_mode, self.ctx_mode = dim, list(shifts), cli_mode, ctx_mode
        self.use_global, self.drop_path, self.layer_scale_init = use_global, drop_path, layer_scale_init
        self.norm = layers.LayerNormalization(epsilon=1e-6, name="norm")
        self.state = layers.Dense(dim, name="state")
        # Two linear 3x3 depthwise convolutions compose into one 5x5 filter.
        self.dw1 = layers.DepthwiseConv2D(3, padding="same", use_bias=False, name="dw1")
        self.dw2 = layers.DepthwiseConv2D(3, padding="same", use_bias=False, name="dw2")
        self.bn = layers.BatchNormalization(name="bn")
        self.local = CliffordInteraction(dim, shifts, cli_mode, ctx_mode, name="local")
        self.glob = (CliffordInteraction(dim, (1, 2), "full", "diff", name="global")
                     if use_global else None)
        self.gate = layers.Dense(dim, name="gate")
        self.seed = keras.random.SeedGenerator(0)

    def build(self, input_shape):
        self.gamma = self.add_weight(
            name="gamma", shape=(self.dim,),
            initializer=keras.initializers.Constant(self.layer_scale_init))
        super().build(input_shape)

    def call(self, x, training=None):
        xn = self.norm(x)
        z_det = self.state(xn)
        z_ctx = ops.silu(self.bn(self.dw2(self.dw1(xn)), training=training))
        g = self.local(z_det, z_ctx)
        if self.glob is not None:
            mean = ops.mean(xn, axis=(1, 2), keepdims=True)
            g = g + self.glob(z_det, ops.broadcast_to(mean, ops.shape(xn)))
        alpha = ops.sigmoid(self.gate(ops.concatenate([xn, g], axis=-1)))
        update = self.gamma * (ops.silu(xn) + alpha * g)
        if training and self.drop_path > 0.0:
            keep = 1.0 - self.drop_path
            u = keras.random.uniform((ops.shape(x)[0], 1, 1, 1), seed=self.seed)
            update = update * ops.floor(keep + u) / keep
        return x + update

    def get_config(self):
        return {**super().get_config(), "dim": self.dim, "shifts": self.shifts,
                "cli_mode": self.cli_mode, "ctx_mode": self.ctx_mode,
                "use_global": self.use_global, "drop_path": self.drop_path,
                "layer_scale_init": self.layer_scale_init}


def build_cliffordnet(num_classes=100, input_shape=(32, 32, 3), dim=128, depth=12,
                      shifts=(1, 2), cli_mode="full", ctx_mode="diff",
                      use_global=False, drop_path_rate=0.3):
    """Isotropic CliffordNet with patch size 2 (one stride-2 3x3 convolution)."""
    inputs = keras.Input(input_shape)
    x = layers.Conv2D(dim, 3, strides=2, padding="same", name="stem")(inputs)
    x = layers.BatchNormalization(name="stem_bn")(x)
    for i in range(depth):
        rate = drop_path_rate * i / max(1, depth - 1)
        x = CliffordBlock(dim, shifts, cli_mode, ctx_mode, use_global, rate, name=f"block{i}")(x)
    x = layers.GlobalAveragePooling2D()(x)
    x = layers.LayerNormalization(epsilon=1e-6, name="head_norm")(x)
    return keras.Model(inputs, layers.Dense(num_classes, name="head")(x))
```


## 17. Experiments That Would Settle the Open Claims

Each item is cheap at CIFAR scale and tests one claim directly.

1. **Does antisymmetry matter?** Replace the wedge branch with its symmetric counterpart, H\_c·C\_{c+s} + C\_c·H\_{c+s}, at the same width. If the antisymmetric version wins, the bivector structure carries information beyond generic cross-channel products.
2. **Matched branch ablation.** Compare inner-only and wedge-only models with 10 shifts against the full model with 5, so all three have the same projection size.
3. **Do cross-channel pairs help?** Run the dot branch at shift 0 only, which is plain GLU-style modulation, with projection width matched.
4. **Modulation baselines in the same backbone.** Swap the interaction for a Conv2Former- or VAN-style block at matched parameters and MACs.
5. **The residual SiLU path.** Remove SiLU(Ĥ) from the gated residual and measure the change.
6. **Differential mode in a wedge-only model.** Predicted to make no difference (Section 5.2); a nonzero effect would point to an implementation difference.
7. **Compute-matched comparison.** Report MACs and throughput, and add CIFAR-adapted CNN baselines and small-data transformers such as CCT, all with the same regularization.
8. **Seeds.** At least three seeds per configuration, with standard deviations.
9. **Scale and tasks.** ImageNet-1K, as the paper plans, and one dense-prediction task at high resolution, where the receptive-field growth of Section 14 will be tested.
10. **The geometric interpretation.** Correlate wedge-branch activations with image-gradient magnitude to test the edge-detection reading.

## 18. Design Principles, Revised

| Principle | Decision | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feature interaction | Sampled geometric product: exact wedge coefficients plus a gated outer-product band | The wedge is genuine but sparse, about 8% of planes; the dot branch is gating, not an inner product |
| Spatial mixing | Two stacked 3×3 depthwise convolutions | A 5×5 filter per block; linear in the number of tokens |
| Channel mixing | Dense state, projection and gate maps | 13D² per block in Lite, quadratic in width; the projection dominates |
| Context mode | Differential | Worth about 1.4 points; acts through the dot branch only |
| Residual update | Gated geometric residual with a SiLU path and LayerScale | Sound; the SiLU path is unablated |
| Global context | Optional gFFN-G | 0.4 to 0.5 points for 30 to 55% more parameters |
| Layout | Isotropic, patch size 2 | High-resolution feature map; compute similar to a CIFAR ResNet-18 |
| FFN | No separate MLP block | The FFN is merged into the interaction, not removed |

**Overall.** CliffordNet is a well-engineered convolutional modulation block with a genuinely geometric addition: exact bivector coefficients on a sparse set of channel planes. The geometric-algebra framing is accurate for the wedge branch and a relabelling for the dot branch and for "algebraic completeness". The efficiency claims hold for parameters, not for compute, and the evidence comes from one small dataset.
